
Predictions were set by the models before each network's post-contingency voltage was computed at each specified target. I used non-overlapping training and testing data to ensure minimal bias. Across the resulting 54 predictions, the mean relative error was $0.4726$ for predictor A and $0.4933$ for predictor B. Both of these predictors use the same inputs (boundary mass and the calibration band width) but differ in what algorithms were used. Predictor A used the parameter-free form $\rho\cdot\hat{q}$ (the predictive density at the limit), while predictor B calculates a trend line across different networks using the same inputs. When predictor A beat B under this unbiased test, it proves that adding a complex, custom calculation does not guarantee any model improvement in comparison to the parameter-free version.  

I also dropped two networks — case57 and case89pegase — early on due to having major issues with them. In case57, the electricity pressure was too low across the whole network, meaning it was not possible to bring any bus above the 0.94 pu threshold under realistic conditions, as at load multiplier 0.0 the minimum post-outage voltage remained 0.9012 pu with 24 buses below the 0.94 pu threshold. In case 89pegase, the network was unable to maintain proper thermal line loading. At load multiplier 0.0, the maximum line loading is 199.37%, meaning that no load-multiplier window falls within a safe thermal line loading threshold, so the case was left out.  

I tested five networks in total, with only three of them completing the full cross-network prediction protocol. There were no claims made about the accuracy of all 54 predictions, and the reported mean relative errors are only the final results from a separate test. This is shown only to prove that fitting a slope on prior networks does not reliably improve predictions on new networks.
