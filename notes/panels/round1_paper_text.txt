  Under-Voltage Contingencies: Safety and
 Throughput Limits Set by Boundary Mass

Abstract
The power system grid should remain resilient following the loss of any individual component.
The N-1 constraint uses an AC power flow solver for each loss event of the grid elements to
determine the voltage of the remaining components. With operators running every combination
and repeatedly testing under different conditions, it becomes computationally expensive. Past work
has either replaced these solves with surrogate models that check a group of cases at once, return
a binary risk classification, or claim no missed unsafe scenarios with only a simple direct current
(DC) model. In this study, I attach a one-sided split-conformal prediction interval to the predicted
minimum post-contingency voltage for a ridge regression and a gradient-boosted model. For each
model, there are three potential outcomes: classify the scenario as safe if the band is above 0.94
per-unit, flag it if the prediction is below the limit, or escalate the situation to the solver if it is a
borderline case. On the IEEE 118-bus AC network at 90% coverage, the gradient-boosted model is
3.29 times faster than the solver but misses 4.72% of violations. For the gradient-boosted model,
first going just under a 1% mean missed rate at a 0.97 coverage target results in a 63.7% escalation
and speedup dropping to roughly 1.58, with error bars going slightly above 1%. In the IEEE 30-bus
system, using a base case that is thermally feasible, where the regenerated 30-bus network has
7.09% of its contingencies in the [0.94, 0.945) boundary band compared to 56.86% for case118, the
gradient-boosted model at 0.97 coverage results in a 5.84±1.25% escalation. These results allow
operators to make an informed decision about whether surrogate screening would be worthwhile
on a specific network.

Keywords: contingency screening, conformal prediction, power-flow surrogate, selective predic-

I    Introduction
Power grids must be able to stay operational even if they lose the functionality of a single element.
The N-1 criterion, for all the elements that might fail, determines whether the calculated voltages
of the remaining components stay within safe operating ranges after an element fails [1]. Normally,
the check itself comes from a physics calculation called an AC power flow solve, run for each
potential contingency. For a complex network with hundreds of elements, the solver becomes
time-consuming and computationally expensive.
    One method to improve performance is to replace the slow physics solver with a fast approx-
imation. However, the fundamental issue with this approach is that it fails undetected, declaring
an unsafe contingency safe, since it only provides a single-point estimate without any confidence
or uncertainty interval [2]. As a result, the operator skips the slow solver and misses the unsafe
scenario altogether. In addition, since the error is asymmetric, predicting too high certifies an un-
safe condition as safe, which is a dangerous operational risk, while predicting too low inefficiently
increases solver time. I add the solver time for escalated cases to the screening gate’s total time to
reflect the true end-to-end cost.
    There have been several approaches when it comes to employing surrogate models for N-1
contingency tests. Manoharan [3] utilized a fast computer model that checks if any single piece of
equipment in the N-1 test is in danger. To confirm its reliability, the system also runs randomized
full solves on some of the equipment it did skip to ensure the model is accurate. Alcántara and
Chatzivasileiadis [4] use an AI foundation model and conformal prediction to create a binary yes/no
switch for post-element outages to determine whether there’s a present risk or not. Christianson et
al. [5] designed an input-convex network that completely removes false negatives, although that
claim is only relevant to DC power flow models, not the AC power flow model my study uses. My
work stands out from these papers in three distinct ways. First, unlike the first two approaches,
I add a three-way gate to each contingency. Second, I specifically check for only under-voltage
issues. Finally, I explain why there must be a certain floor for escalation.

II    Background
A per-unit (pu) voltage normalizes power grid values into simple decimals (where 1.0 is nominal),
where 0.94 per unit indicates a 6% under-voltage. Utilities must keep voltage within a band around
nominal, and this study flags any part of the grid that fails the test against an under-voltage floor
of 0.94 per unit. The N-0 condition represents the current state of the grid without any equipment
outages, in which all voltages are above a minimum limit. I refer to this pre-outage state of the grid
as a base case. However, if a single piece of equipment fails, the rest of the network must absorb
the extra load, which could cause the voltage at one or more buses to go under the safe 0.94 pu
floor. N-1 screening simulates these failures and detects any violations of the under-voltage floor
before they happen. In this study, I do not check for overvoltage, although 73.1% of converged N-1
rows the highest bus voltage is over 1.05 pu, or the upper bound of the acceptable voltage band.
Also, the gate only bounds one predicted scalar, so adding more factors would require their own
respective target and bands. I cannot check for thermal loading because case118 assigns every line
the same 9,900 MVA rating, which is a placeholder value that is not realistic, so the test would pass
every case regardless of conditions [7].
     An AC power flow solver is used to get the final voltages across a network after a single-
element failure. Due to the math being complex and non-linear, it takes around 9 milliseconds for
one scenario on an Apple M5 processor, and the time multiplies across the whole grid. By contrast,
a prediction from a machine learning model can take a fraction of a millisecond for predicting
the single minimum voltage out of the whole grid. Classical fast screens rank power system
contingencies without running a full solver [6]. While this indicates which equipment is subject to
the most danger when there’s an equipment failure, it fails to provide a numerical minimum voltage.
     I study the IEEE 118-bus system [7], a standard electrical power test case with 118 buses (the
junctions where voltage is measured), 173 lines, and 13 transformers. Since the contingencies
include only lines and transformers, there are 186 total pieces of equipment that are subject to
failure. Each contingency subjects a single line or transformer to failure. Some base cases are also
missing a generator before the contingency, so those rows have two elements out after it, which I
describe in Section III. I use pandapower [8], a Python library used to model power systems, for
solving post-outage voltages while ensuring specific generator reactive power limits are enforced.

III      Method

III.1    Dataset

I determined the load levels by scaling the initial network load by multipliers. For Independent
mode (750 bases), I multiplied the load by multipliers ranging from 1.0 to 1.12. For Regional
mode (750 bases), I did the same but from 0.9009 to 1.2310 (43.91% outside the 1.0 to 1.12 target
range), while reactive power scales across all 1,500 bases range from 0.8156 to 1.4083 (to account
for stochastic variations in the random power factor). The base cases were kept if the minimum
pre-outage voltage was above 0.94 per unit. Before this check, I removed one generator (never the
slack) 30% of the time. Since weaker grids failed the check more often, only 374 of the 1,500 kept
bases (24.93%) have a generator that is out of service. Each accepted base case was put through
a simulation of 186 contingencies representing single-element failures, followed by an alternating-
current power flow calculation to determine the minimum bus voltage after each contingency. The
total sample contained 1,500 base cases and 280,500 rows, including the base cases. Out of those
rows, 1,500 were N-0 base rows that were excluded because they aren’t contingencies, while the
other 279,000 rows are N-1 contingencies. Out of those 279,000, 45 were removed due to failing
to converge, resulting in 278,955 converged rows. Minimum post-contingency bus voltages ranged
from 0.7179 to 0.9603 per unit, and 17.48% of the observations violated the voltage limit. I also
tested the published IEEE 30-bus system, where the published base case is at 111.83% line loading,
which is discussed in Section V. Because that loading is unrealistic, I also regenerated the 30-bus
network by keeping only the base cases that have the maximum line loading of at most 100% and
lowering the load-multiplier window to 0.87–0.99, which brought the maximum base-case line
loading down to just under 100%. Since there are 41 rather than 186 branches, the 1,500 bases
produce 61,500 contingency scenarios, all of which were solved successfully.
      The previous implementation of the dataset generator used a lower bound of 0.94 per unit
for voltage set points and truncated any set points that fell below that lower bound value at the
threshold. That resulted in a cluster of samples near 0.940000 ± 1 × 10−9 per unit, which accounted
for 35.17% of the converged rows, due mainly to bus 76, which was a generator bus and had a base
voltage set point of 0.943 per unit. I discovered the problem after inverting the escalation versus
band width relation, which returned unattainable accuracy requirements. After modifying the code

III.2    Surrogates

The surrogate predicts the minimum voltage following an outage based on the per-bus loads, how
generators are set up, baseline voltages, and the failed equipment. The two algorithms used in
this study are a ridge linear regression model using standardized inputs and a histogram-based
gradient-boosted tree ensemble [9]. The data is split into 60%, 20%, and 20% sets using different
base scenarios for training, calibration, and test sets, respectively. To tune the specific variables
for each model, I split the training set again into three smaller parts: one to fit the model, one to
calculate the band width ˆ

sets when choosing variable values. I kept whichever variable values skipped the most solves while
still missing 1% or fewer of the violations, and I repeated the whole process with five different
random splits. I tested my models against two baselines: persistence, which predicts the pre-outage

III.4     Three-way gate

For each contingency, the gate performs the following tests for the band and the point prediction
compared with the limit 𝐿 = 0.94:

    • Certify (skip the solver): 𝑝ˆ − 𝑞ˆ ≥ 𝐿, meaning that the lower edge of the band exceeds the
        limit.

    • Flag (skip the solver): 𝑝ˆ < 𝐿, meaning that the point prediction itself is below the limit.

    • Escalate (invoke the solver) otherwise, that is, when the band straddles the limit.

The flagging test employs the point prediction, since the lower edge of the one-sided band has no
upper edge (Fig. 1). Because the exact solver is invoked on escalations only, the net speedup has to
account for that additional time taken:

Here 𝑛 is the total number of contingencies, 𝑡solve is the solver time per case (set at 9.14 ms, which

IV.3     The boundary layer sets a floor on escalation

Most of the contingencies lie within 0.005 per unit of the boundary, resulting in a significant
number of escalations. While the methodology of this research is similar to classical reject-option
results, it is not a direct application of it. Chow [15] studied a Bayes-optimal classifier that rejects
whenever the maximum posterior probability is smaller than a set threshold, and he proved that the
rejection threshold is the local slope of the error–reject curve. In addition to this, margin conditions
[16, 17] quantify the rate at which the probability mass decreases in a shrinking band around the
class boundary. Both have the posterior confidence of being close to a class boundary as their
main similarity, while the proposed escalation region is one band width wide above a fixed voltage
limit. Therefore, the methodology carries over, not the underlying theorem. Fig. 4 displays the
distribution of voltages after the contingencies. Of all converged cases, 56.86% fall in the narrow
range [0.94, 0.945), slightly above the threshold, and 17.48% fall below the limit as violations. In
addition, there is not a single 0.001 per-unit bin that accounts for more than about 14% of the whole
dataset. Certain buses account for extreme events, with bus 76 being the weakest node, showing

V     Discussion and limitations
Calibrated uncertainty, as well as making the decision of either outsourcing the problem or not to
a more complex model [20] and providing the model with multiple choices [21, 22, 15, 23], are
proven methods. In this study, two things differ from that literature. First, I fall back to an exact
solver instead of a more complex model, so the escalated case can be resolved, and second, I also
explain why the speedup is capped, which is because most of the data is very close to the limit.
    My approach differs from Manoharan’s audited population control [3], which is itself a form
of distribution-free risk control [2]. That method uses a bound for the proportion of skipped cases
that are unsafe, while the gate uses a margin of error that is subtracted from each prediction. This
establishes the lower bound because, in split conformal prediction, I can be correct on average
but not necessarily inside the band. If one demands perfect correctness with a limited amount of
calibration data, then the prediction intervals become extremely wide, to the point where it becomes
useless [18]. Every base case generates 186 related contingencies, and therefore, the coverage rates
are measured averages and not an assurance for a single contingency.
    The key findings obtained during the experiments relate to a network with a realistic topology
and synthetic loads and generation. This escalation floor is set by how the distribution of the data
occurs in relation to the 0.94 pu limit. For instance, in a regeneration of the IEEE 30-bus network
that is thermally feasible in the base case, 7.09% of contingencies fall just above the 0.94 pu limit,
while another 15.40% fall below it. For case118, 56.86% fall just above the limit, and 17.48% fall
below it. The original 30-bus system is thermally unrealistic, since it has 111.83% line loading
in its base case. The gradient-boosted model with a coverage target of 0.97 gives 5.84±1.25%
escalations and misses 0.76±0.20% of violations. All five splits are under 1% at 0.97, but not at
0.96, where the mean is 0.91±0.22%, with only two of five being under this limit. I use 0.94 pu
as a conservative choice. Only 86 of the 1,500 base cases are above 0.95 pu, and increasing the


                                                                                                   13
threshold to 0.95 pu leads to a ridge escalation of 1.38±0.33%. In other words, it finds everything
hazardous.
      All metrics reported use the sampled N-1 population, in which 374 of the 1,500 base cases
(24.93%, or 69,532 rows) already have one generator out of service before the branch outage. These
are still N-1 contingencies, since each one only removes one branch. Moreover, mathematical proof
is not capable of guaranteeing the safety of N-2 outages where two branches fail together because
the use of conformal prediction assumes that the data is exchangeable with the calibration set [11].
Weighted conformal prediction relaxes exchangeability in an instance where there is a shift in the
covariate distribution and the likelihood ratio between the two distributions is either known or
can be estimated [19]. However, this ratio only exists when the shifted distribution is absolutely
continuous relative to the calibration distribution, which is not the case for N-2 outages. Also, I
only checked if the final voltage was too low, not for thermal overheating, over-voltage, or whether
or not the grid crashed in the first place, which is called dynamic stability. On the regenerated
30-bus network, 21.5% of the contingencies still go over 100% line loading (up to 145.8%), which
the gate does not check. The band only counts instances where the model is wrong and not how
badly it fails. In addition, the worst case, at 0.0915 per unit, represents a case of sudden collapse
where the generator at that particular worst-case scenario’s weakest bus reaches the limit of reactive
power and can no longer control the voltage. The model cannot foresee this event just from the
pre-outage data. Lastly, all of the testing data were obtained by making slight modifications to the
same pre-contingency grid setup. Therefore, there is some uncertainty as to how often the models
should be recalibrated due to changes in the seasons and changes in demand.


V.1     Physics-feature ablation

In the specific ablations, I intentionally left out the aggregate loading (agg loading), so the feature
matrix does not have any direct information about line loading. Although the total demand is
concealed from the model, the feature matrix contains pre-outage voltages (vm0 *) and other
physics features tested below. All results below are from tests without agg loading.
      I tested five different setups: baseline, +F1 (add pre-outage branch flows on the outaged
element), +F1+F2 (add F1 and the full pre-outage loading vector), +F1+F3 (add F1 and the LODF



                                                                                                    14
rows), and +F1+F2+F3+F4 (add F1, F2, F3, and electrical distance F4). For each configuration,
I trained ridge and histogram-based gradient boosting and conducted tests with hyperparameters
re-searched per configuration and with them fixed. I report mean absolute error (MAE) to measure
prediction accuracy and also include seed standard deviation to account for randomized seeds
starting at different points. The best configurations (with hyperparameters re-searched) had a
MAE of 0.00371 ± 0.00010 for ridge and 0.00153 ± 0.00011 for histgb. The additional features
contribute no meaningful increase in performance compared to the basic ones, as it is less than the
seed standard deviation.
    The one negative outcome obtained here is that including F2 (the entire vector of pre-outage
loadings) worsens ridge’s results. When comparing the model with baseline versus with +F1+F2, the
ridge MAE increases by +10.82% (baseline 0.003754 ± 0.000133 to +F1+F2 0.004161 ± 0.000247
in the re-searched runs). This increase in error is greater than seed variability regardless of whether
the hyperparameters are adjusted or not.
    To investigate further, I ran a three-arm permutation control: the basic model, the model with
real F1 data, and a model with shuffled F1 data. In a quick single-seed histgb check, the MAE values
were 0.0015638 for baseline, 0.0015539 for real F1, and 0.0015300 for shuffled. The shuffled arm
was lower than real F1 by (2.39 × 10−5 ), but this difference is small enough that it falls within
normal seed variation, as the histgb seed standard deviation is (≈ 7.58 × 10−5 ). In addition, I
also conducted a leakage audit to ensure the data was not training the model from previous tests.
The audit recreates the four F1 columns (pre p mw, pre q mvar, pre loading percent, pre i ka)
from scratch across 25 different scenarios, and everything matched up, suggesting there was no
accidental data leakage. Since the input data varies across different scenarios, I can conclude that
any change in values from the audit was not due to constant features and was from dynamic data.
    Since shuffling the F1 data did not significantly decrease performance, the data itself does not
contribute meaningful real-world physical information that would boost performance beyond the
existing per-element encoding in the branch one-hot representation.

VI     Conclusion and future work
My models can predict N-1 under-voltage contingencies on the IEEE 118-bus system. On this
network, safety and speed are inversely related, though the 30-bus result shows that this depends on
data distribution relative to the limit. At 90% coverage, both methods perform very fast, yet they
miss too many violations to be considered safe. To make sure that the missed ratio approaches 1%
on average, almost two-thirds of the cases have to be escalated, resulting in a speed-up factor of
1.6. The main reason is that most of the cases fall very close to the limit. The bands often straddle
this limit and then must escalate to the solver as the result is uncertain. What this study contributes
here is the reasoning for the existence of such a floor, and a warning that people should expect less
speed-up from a surrogate model that is calibrated to be safe on average over the N-1 distribution.
Future work involves examining multi-element outages and more networks to determine whether
speedup depends on the amount of data that lies near the threshold.
